01 - framing

The size of the pot does not change the spoonful

You are cooking soup. To find out whether it needs salt you take one spoonful. You do not take a proportional spoonful - you do not take a bigger spoon because the pot is bigger. One spoonful settles the question for a bowl and for a cauldron alike, provided you stirred.

This is the whole of sampling, and it is the part that feels wrong. The intuition most people carry is that a poll of 1,000 tells you very little about 300 million, because 1,000 is such a tiny fraction. That intuition is measuring the wrong thing. What buys you precision is the absolute size of the sample, not its share of the population. A sample of 1,000 is about as informative about a country of 300 million as it is about a town of 30,000.

Remember

I thought a bigger population needed a bigger sample. Actually the population size drops out almost entirely - what you are buying with each extra person is a smaller spread, and the pot has nothing to do with it.

02 - the mechanism

Your answer is a random variable, not a number

Draw a sample, average it, and you get a number. It feels solid. But had you knocked on a different set of doors that morning you would have got a different number - so the thing you are holding is not really a number at all. It is one draw from a distribution of numbers you could have got.

Fig. 1 - one population, three samples, three different answers. The dashed curve is the shape they scatter in.
the sample mean
$$\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i$$
reading it
  • x̅ - "x-bar", the average of the people you actually asked. Your answer.
  • n - how many you asked. The sample size.
  • xᵢ - the value you got from person i. One measurement.
  • Σ - "add up all of them", from the first person to the n-th.
  • the whole expression - add up everyone you measured, divide by how many there were. Nothing more than an average - the interesting part is that this average is itself random.

Because x̅ is random, asking "is my answer right?" is the wrong question. It is almost certainly not exactly right. The answerable question is how far off is it likely to be - and that has a formula, which is §03.

Remember

The sample mean is not an approximation of a number, it is a draw from a distribution. Everything useful in statistics comes from knowing the shape of that distribution without ever seeing it.

03 - the shape of the trade

Four times the work buys half the error

The spread of that distribution has a name - the standard error - and a formula so short it hides how much it decides.

standard error of the mean
$$SE = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$$
reading it
  • SE - the typical distance between your answer and the truth. Small SE, trustworthy answer.
  • σ - "sigma", how spread out the population is. If everyone answers nearly the same, σ is small and one spoonful settles it.
  • n - the sample size, as before.
  • √n - the square root of the sample size. This is the whole story, see below.
  • the whole expression - your error is the population's own messiness, divided by the square root of how many you asked. Note what is absent: the population size never appears.

The square root is the cruel part. To halve your error you do not need twice the sample, you need four times it. To cut it to a tenth, a hundred times. Precision gets expensive fast, and it is why polls cluster around a thousand people rather than a hundred thousand: the first thousand buys most of what is affordable to buy.

Remember

Precision improves with the square root of effort, so the returns flatten almost immediately. The last doubling of accuracy always costs more than everything that came before it.

04 - the failure mode

Stirring is the assumption that actually breaks

Everything above rested on one word in §01: stirred. Every member of the population had to be equally likely to end up in your spoon. When that holds, more sample means less error. When it does not, more sample means a more confident wrong answer.

Fig. 3 - the standard error only describes the left-hand picture. Nothing in the formula can see the gap on the right.

The 1936 Literary Digest poll asked 2.4 million people and got the US election badly wrong, because it drew names from telephone and car registries in a year when owning either meant you were rich. A sample of 2.4 million with a tilt beat a sample of a few thousand without one - and lost. The bias term does not appear in SE, so no amount of n reports it to you. You have to reason about how the sample was drawn, which is judgement, not arithmetic.

Remember

The standard error measures noise, never tilt. A large sample tells you your answer is stable, and says nothing at all about whether it is pointed at the truth.

In one breath

A sample is a spoonful, and the pot's size never enters the question - what you carry away is not a number but a draw from a distribution, whose spread is the population's own messiness divided by the square root of how many you asked, so precision improves but only at quadratically rising cost, and the entire arrangement rests on the soup having been stirred, because the moment it was not, every extra spoonful buys you nothing but confidence in the wrong answer.
